# Spoken English practice

Read me first. Everything needed to run the programme is here. There is nothing to
write and nothing to decide along the way.

**Everything runs in English** — the page, the agent, the group. The only Spanish that
appears is the cue side of an exercise: the sentence you have to turn into English. If
that were in English too, the drill would stop being production and become recognition,
which is the thing that doesn't work.

---

## What this is

A WhatsApp group of three to five Spanish speakers training to **speak** English, not
to understand it. A text-only agent publishes each session on a web page, launches a
quick round at midday, and collects who did what in the evening.

The target is specific: **stop freezing in the middle of a sentence.** Not speaking
faster, not losing the accent, not getting rid of the "um"s.

---

## Getting started

1. Host `lesson.html` somewhere the group can open with a link and the agent can edit.
   Session 1 is already loaded.
2. Give the agent `AGENT.md` and `PLAN-MONTH-1.md`. That's all it needs.
3. Have everyone read the **Method** tab before starting. Three minutes, and it
   prevents half the questions.
4. Have everyone pick the AI they'll grade themselves with.
5. Post the welcome message (it's at the end of `PLAN-MONTH-1.md`).
6. Session 1 opens with the checkpoint: two minutes recorded, unprepared. Without it,
   nobody will know a month from now whether this worked.

---

## The rhythm

**Every other day: Monday, Wednesday and Friday.** Twelve sessions a month. Each day
has its fixed activity, so nobody has to decide anything.

| Day | Activity | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | **Speaking** | The same topic five times in a row, out loud |
| Wednesday | **Vocabulary** | Ten new expressions and a timed sprint |
| Friday | **Writing** | Write against the clock, then speak on the same topic |
| Saturday | Call | Half an hour live, in English |

All three sessions end the same way: the grammar trap, the quick round and the
wrap-up. Vocabulary and writing also close with two minutes of speaking, so a whole
week never goes by without anyone opening their mouth.

**Twelve sessions is not laziness dressed up.** The dose that worked in the research is
small: twelve narrations were enough to improve speaking on new topics, and here people
do twenty. The programmes that failed are the high-volume ones — thirty-two hours of
pronunciation training, sixteen weeks of shadowing.

---

## How the demand climbs

| Week | Speaking | Writing |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Narrative · 90s | 200 words |
| 2 | Descriptive · 2 min | 250 words |
| 3 | Explanatory · 2 min | 275 words |
| 4 | Argumentative · 2 min | 300 words |

Narrating is the cheapest: the order of events holds up anyone who stalls. Describing
takes that support away. Explaining demands precision. Arguing demands taking a
position and conceding a point at once, which is the hardest thing to formulate. That's
why it comes last.

**Sessions 1 and 12: checkpoint.** The same two-minute recording, unprepared. It's the
only thing that will say whether the month was worth it.

**Month 2 comes from the gaps collected**, not from another list. At session 12 the
agent hands each person their own "got stuck on" lines back.

---

## Who grades

**Each person's own AI, not the agent.** Every session the page carries a prompt: you
leave ChatGPT or whichever you use listening, and it gives you a score out of ten and
two concrete things to fix. You type that into the page and it goes straight into the
report.

The prompts grade **one thing only**: whether you stall mid-sentence. They forbid any
comment on accent, pronunciation or filled pauses, and they ask the AI to compare you
against yourself, not against a native speaker.

Scores are never compared between people and never appear in the weekly summary.

## The six rules that don't bend

If something breaks over time, it will be one of these.

1. **The agent never corrects English and never comments on scores.** Correction lives
   in each person's own AI, with the prompt on the page.
2. **The five rounds are the same topic and the same length.** The clock does not get
   shorter on the last ones.
3. **All five rounds, not three.** Mid-sentence pauses keep improving through the
   fourth.
4. **Every session ends by speaking.** Even the vocabulary and writing ones.
5. **The agent touches nothing outside the editable block** and never deletes old
   sessions: the route, the review and the sprint depend on them.
6. **Nobody leaves the group for not showing up.** No private messages, no calling out
   absences. Explicit pressure makes people leave instead of coming back.

---

## Four things that sound sensible and aren't

These are measured. The detail and the sources are in `docs/protocol.html`.

- **Adding more podcasts or shows as homework.** The effect on production is close to
  zero. They keep the base; they don't train speaking.
- **Letting the agent correct.** Handing someone the right answer works half as well as
  pointing at the error and making them try again, and a basic model does the first.
- **Correcting pronunciation or filled pauses.** Thirty-two hours of pronunciation
  training changed nothing in spontaneous speech. Native speakers produce about six
  filled pauses a minute.
- **Studying every day when the group stalls.** The problem is never the dose. It's
  people stopping.

---

## What to expect

| Timeframe | What's reasonable |
|---|---|
| 2–3 weeks | First measurable change in pausing. Getting going feels easier |
| 1–2 months | Mid-sentence pauses drop on new topics |
| 5+ months | The real change. One month is the first step |

Two warnings so nobody gets discouraged for the wrong reason. **Speed won't rise much**:
this training improves pausing and doesn't move speed, and that's expected. And
**grammar improves slowly**, through correction, not on its own.
